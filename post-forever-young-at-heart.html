What If Staying Young
Was Never About Your Face?

August 23, 2026
By Faye Livia
9 min read

There's a woman at every party who is somehow the most alive person in the room — and she's rarely the youngest one in it. Turns out there's a name for what she has, and a surprising amount of science behind it.

You know her. Maybe you are her. She's 58 and just signed up for a pottery class where she will definitely make something lopsided. She laughs with her whole face. She asks follow-up questions like she actually wants to know the answer. Meanwhile, somewhere in the same room, there's a 29-year-old who sighs before answering basically anything, has opinions about how things "used to be better," and treats spontaneity like a scheduling conflict.

Age, it turns out, was never really the variable. And for once, the science backs up the greeting-card wisdom.

The short answer

"Forever young at heart" isn't a personality vibe — it's a real, measurable thing researchers call subjective age: how old you feel versus how old you actually are. Most adults over 40 feel roughly 20% younger than their real age, and that gap turns out to matter more for your health than you'd expect.

The Number That Started This Whole Conversation

In one of the more delightful studies in psychology, researchers surveyed nearly 1,500 adults ranging from 20 to 97 years old and asked them, essentially, how old they felt on the inside. The pattern that emerged was almost too tidy to be true.

20%
Roughly how much younger the average adult over 40 feels compared to their actual age — and the gap tends to get bigger, not smaller, the older people get. Under 25, people tend to feel slightly older than they are, as if rushing to catch up to adulthood. Somewhere around the mid-twenties, the current reverses, and most of us spend the rest of our lives feeling pleasantly behind schedule.

So if you're 47 and still catch yourself surprised by your own reflection because some part of you is still expecting to see 33 — congratulations, you're not in denial. You're exactly on trend.

What "Young at Heart" Actually Means, Scientifically

Strip away the greeting-card version and you're left with a specific, recognizable set of traits — the kind researchers can actually measure.

01

Playfulness

Finding joy in genuinely small things — a good pun, a dog in sunglasses, a perfectly ridiculous meme — isn't a lack of sophistication. It's a working sense of humor, and it's linked to the same dopamine-driven reward pathways that make novelty feel good in the first place. The brain doesn't stop enjoying delight with age. Most of us just stop letting it.

02

Curiosity

This is one of the "Big Five" personality traits psychologists actually study — openness to experience. It's the drive to learn something new instead of defaulting to what's already familiar, and it does something concrete in the brain: novelty and learning trigger neuroplasticity, the process by which your brain builds new connections. Translation — staying curious is, quite literally, a workout for your brain's ability to keep changing.

03

Resilience

Bouncing back from a hard week instead of letting it calcify into permanent cynicism is a skill, not a personality default — and it's one of the clearest markers separating people who stay young at heart from people who quietly grow bitter. It's less about avoiding hard things and more about not letting hard things become the whole story.

04

Open-mindedness

The willingness to adapt to change and sit with new ideas instead of reflexively rejecting them. Its opposite — cognitive rigidity, a hardening into fixed opinions and routines — is one of the most consistent predictors researchers use to describe someone who has, psychologically, gotten "old" regardless of their actual age.

Nobody has ever met someone who felt young and seemed miserable about it. Curiosity and joy just don't tend to travel with dread.

The Slightly Wild Part: Feeling Older Might Actually Age You

Here's where this stops being a nice idea and starts being a genuinely strange finding. Researchers didn't just measure how old people felt — they followed those people for years afterward to see what happened.

People who felt older than their actual age were more likely to develop depression, cognitive decline, and even dementia over time — while people who felt younger tended to stay sharper, healthier, and live longer. The feeling wasn't just a symptom of aging. It was, unnervingly, a predictor of it.

Long-running studies tracking older adults over time have found that an older subjective age is tied to higher rates of inflammation, depressive symptoms, and stress reactivity — and a meaningfully higher risk of hospitalization and earlier mortality. A younger subjective age, by contrast, tracks with better physical health and sharper cognitive function years down the line. Some of this is almost certainly behavioral — people who feel younger tend to stay more physically active and more socially connected, both of which independently protect the brain and body. But the association holds up even after researchers account for that, which suggests the feeling itself isn't just along for the ride. It may be doing some of the driving.

The Quieter Traits Nobody Puts on a Mug

Alongside the playful, headline-grabbing traits, there's a second, softer layer to staying young at heart — less about energy, more about how you hold yourself.

Living in the Present
not rehearsing the past or the worst-case future

Children are famously terrible at worrying about next Tuesday. Staying young at heart means borrowing a little of that — letting the present moment be the main event instead of a waiting room for regret or anxiety.

Self-Acceptance
the "unconditional love" version, minus the mysticism

Children accept themselves before the world teaches them not to. Young-at-heart adults tend to have found their way back to that — liking themselves without requiring a permission slip from anyone else first.

Hope, Practiced Deliberately
a real, studied psychological trait — dispositional optimism

Not blind faith that everything will work out — a trained tendency to expect that it might. This one has genuine research behind it: a study of over 97,000 women found the most optimistic had a 30% lower risk of dying from heart disease than the most pessimistic, over years of follow-up.

Letting Go of "Serious"
releasing the performance of being important

A specific kind of relief that comes from no longer needing every room to see you as impressive, composed, or in control. Children have never once worried about looking cool. There's a lesson in that most of us spend decades unlearning.

Okay, But How Do You Actually Do This?

None of this requires quitting your job to backpack through your twenties again. It mostly requires lowering the stakes on purpose.

Five small ways to bring the "young at heart" traits back online Pick one. Start smaller than feels necessary.
  • Do one thing badly on purpose. A beginner dance class, a new recipe, an instrument you'll never master. The goal isn't skill — it's practicing the feeling of being a beginner again, without the pressure.
  • Say yes to the plan you'd normally talk yourself out of. The impromptu one, the slightly inconvenient one. That's usually where the good stories live.
  • Ask a genuinely curious question in conversation. Not the polite kind — the kind where you actually want to know the answer and it shows.
  • Let yourself be delighted by something small without editing the reaction. The good song, the ridiculous dog, the perfect sunset. Feel it out loud instead of internally noting it as "nice."
  • Retire one "at my age" sentence this week. Notice how often that phrase shows up as a reason not to do something, and question it once, just to see what happens.
A quick, honest caveat

Subjective age is a real and meaningfully studied trait, but it's not a substitute for actual health care, and it isn't purely a mindset switch — genetics, chronic illness, and circumstance all shape how someone feels day to day, and none of that is a personal failing. This is about tilting the odds in a direction that's genuinely within your control, not a claim that attitude alone determines outcomes.

What if the goal was never to look young — just to stay curious enough to keep feeling like it?

Nobody is asking you to pretend you're 23. The actual invitation is smaller and, honestly, more fun: stay a little more delighted, a little more willing to be bad at new things, a little less committed to being impressive. The research suggests your brain and body will thank you for it — but even if the science didn't hold up, it still sounds like a better way to spend a Tuesday.
