How to Care Less About People You Love — and Live More at Ease

November 22, 2026
By Faye Livia
9 min read

I spent years reminding my mother to eat three meals a day. Then I stopped — not gracefully, not as a strategy, just out of exhaustion. And something happened that I'm still slightly annoyed about.

My mother would spend an entire day in the garden if nobody interrupted her. Out with the chickens by early morning, and then it's suddenly four in the afternoon and she hasn't eaten since breakfast, and when I ask she says she wasn't hungry, which is not the same thing as having eaten.

So I reminded her. About lunch. About moving her body a little — nothing strenuous, just something. About reading and writing a bit each day, because I'd read what happens to a brain that stops being asked to do anything difficult, and because she's the only mother I have.

And she had an excuse for all of it. Every time. Beautiful, inexhaustible, faintly creative excuses. The light was good so she had to finish the row. Her knee. Later. Tomorrow. She'd eat properly at dinner. She was fine, why was I always going on.

I want to be honest about how that felt, because I think it's the part people skip. It didn't feel like devotion. It felt like being a nag in my own house, listening to my voice come out in a tone I didn't like and couldn't stop, asking a grown woman whether she'd had lunch for what must have been the four hundredth time.

Then I stopped. Not in a considered, boundaried, therapeutic way. I just ran out of whatever the reminders were running on.

And things changed.

Not everything. But enough that I had to go and find out why — because the explanation I'd been carrying around turned out to be wrong in a way I found genuinely startling.

What I Assumed, and What's Actually True

My assumption was the obvious one: nagging doesn't work. All those reminders had accomplished nothing, and when I let go, nothing was lost.

That's not what the research says. The research says something considerably more uncomfortable.

There's a body of work in relationship psychology on what's called health-related social control — the ordinary business of people in close relationships monitoring and trying to steer each other's habits. Eat something. Sit down. See a doctor. The foundational finding, from Megan Lewis and Karen Rook in 1999, is what researchers call a dual effect, and it is not the tidy result anyone wants.

Social control did predict better health behaviour. Less of the harmful stuff, more of the helpful stuff. It worked.

It also predicted more psychological distress. Reliably. At the same time.

Both
Being monitored and pressured about your health does shift behaviour — and raises distress. The two findings arrive together. This is the part that changed how I understood four hundred reminders.

Sit with what that means for a minute. The reminders weren't useless. They were probably nudging her, slightly, in the direction I wanted. And they were simultaneously making both of us miserable — her, because she was being managed; me, because I had become the manager.

I hadn't been failing to persuade her. I'd been succeeding a little, at a price neither of us could keep paying.

That reframe undid something in me. I'd been treating the exhaustion as the cost of not yet having found the right words. It was never a words problem. It was a method problem, and the method was expensive by design.

Why the Excuse Exists

The second piece explains the excuses, and it's older.

In 1966 the psychologist Jack Brehm described psychological reactance: when people sense that their freedom to choose is being restricted, they experience an unpleasant motivational arousal, and they act to get that freedom back. Often by doing precisely the opposite of what's being urged. Not out of spite. Out of something closer to self-preservation.

Which reframes every excuse I ever heard.

The knee, the light, the row that had to be finished — those weren't laziness, and they weren't really about lunch. They were freedom being restored. My mother had wanted, at some point, to be a person who ate properly and moved her body. Then I started reminding her, and the whole thing quietly changed ownership. It stopped being her reason and became my requirement, and once it was my requirement, refusing it was the only way left to prove the choice was still hers.

People will defend their autonomy over their own interests. Every time. It isn't irrational — for most of us, being the author of our own life matters more than optimising it, and a person who has lost most of their other authority will defend the small remaining territory ferociously.

I had turned her lunch into a referendum on whether she was still in charge of herself. Of course she kept voting.

What Caring Less Actually Is

Here's where I want to be careful, because "care less" sounds like advice to become colder, and that's the opposite of what happened.

What I put down wasn't the caring. It was the managing. Those are different operations, and we've collapsed them so thoroughly that dropping one feels like abandoning the other.

Self-determination theory — Deci and Ryan's framework, the one most of modern motivation research runs on — calls the alternative autonomy support. You stay involved. You offer information, help, presence. And you leave the decision genuinely with the person, including the decisions you think are wrong. Autonomy-supportive relating is consistently associated with better long-term maintenance of a behaviour than pressure is, and there's even work linking autonomy support in close relationships to measurably lower blood pressure over time.

The mechanism isn't mysterious. When the reason belongs to you, you keep doing it after the person reminding you leaves the room.

01

Say it once, then genuinely drop it

Once is information. Four times is pressure, and the fourth time undoes the first three. This is the whole practice, and it's much harder than it reads, because the second and third reminders are where the anxiety goes.

02

Make the thing available, not mandatory

I stopped asking whether she'd eaten. I started leaving food out where she'd walk past it. The difference between an offer and an instruction is whether refusing it costs anything.

03

Stop asking for compliance reports

"Did you do your walk?" is surveillance wearing the costume of interest, and everyone can tell. The check-in is where autonomy support quietly turns back into control.

04

Let it be visibly, boringly optional

Not sighing counts. Not going quiet counts. If the cost of saying no is your mood for the evening, then it was never optional and the reactance stays exactly where it was.

What changed, in our house, was small and unglamorous. She started coming in for lunch more often than she used to — not always, and I'd be overselling it to call it a transformation. But the fight went out of it. It stopped being a thing we did to each other every afternoon.

And the reading and the writing? Mostly still not happening. That one I've released, and I've had to make actual peace with it rather than pretending to.

The Line That Isn't Negotiable

One important caveat, because I'd hate for this to be read as blanket permission to look away.

Caring less applies to preferences and habits. It does not apply to safety.

Skipping lunch in favour of the tomatoes is a habit. A sudden change in how someone is functioning, signs of cognitive decline, falls, real malnutrition, untreated symptoms, medication going unmanaged — none of that is let-them territory. That's territory for a doctor, quickly, whether or not the person is enthusiastic about it. Backing off from someone's exercise routine and backing off when something is genuinely wrong are different acts, and telling them apart is part of caring well rather than an exception to it.

What I released was the belief that another adult's ordinary choices were mine to engineer. I didn't stop watching.

How to Actually Put It Down

Caring Less, In Practice

Expect this to feel worse before it feels free. That's not a sign you were right to be managing things.

  • Name what's actually yours. Write the list down. Most of it isn't. The clarity is unpleasant and immediately useful.
  • Give the impulse somewhere else to go. The urge to remind doesn't vanish because you've decided it should — it needs rerouting. A walk, a line in a notebook, a message to a friend instead of to the person.
  • Watch your tone, not just your words. A sigh is a reminder. So is a pointed silence. Reactance responds to pressure in any format.
  • Expect a hard fortnight. You've removed the outlet for the worry without removing the worry. It settles, but not in the first week.
  • Don't announce it. "I'm not going to nag you anymore" is a manoeuvre, and it will be heard as one. Just stop.
  • Let the released hours be actually yours. This connects to slow living in a fast world more than it looks — there's no point buying back your attention and then leaving it lying around.
  • Judge it on your life, not on their behaviour. The measure is whether your days got lighter. If you're still scoring their compliance, you haven't let go, you've just gone quiet.

There's one more thing, and it's the honest part.

Caring less did not turn my mother into someone who asks whether I've eaten. That exchange still runs in one direction, and I've stopped expecting the accounts to balance. What changed isn't her. It's that my own afternoons are no longer organised around a negotiation I was never going to win.

That's the freedom, and it's quieter than the phrase "live a free and happy life" suggests. It isn't lightness. It's the specific relief of having handed something back to the person it belonged to, and discovering how much room was underneath.

She's still out there with the chickens. I've stopped counting her meals. We're both, I think, slightly better off.

You are allowed to love someone and not be in charge of them.

Caring less isn't coldness, and it isn't giving up. It's the discovery that managing another adult costs you both something and buys almost nothing — and that when you set down the steering, what's left is the part that was actually love. Say it once. Leave the food out. Go and have your afternoon.

Questions About Caring Less

It means withdrawing control, not withdrawing care. You keep noticing, keep loving, keep being available. What you put down is the belief that another adult's choices are your responsibility to manage, and the daily effort of trying to steer them. In practice caring less usually looks like saying a thing once and then genuinely dropping it, rather than saying it four times in escalating tones.

Two things are happening. The first is psychological reactance, described by Jack Brehm in 1966: when people feel their freedom to choose is being restricted, they experience an uncomfortable motivational arousal and act to restore that freedom, often by doing the opposite of what was urged. The second is subtler. Research on health-related social control in close relationships has found a dual effect — being monitored and pressured does sometimes shift behaviour, but it reliably raises psychological distress at the same time. So reminders are not simply useless. They often work a little, and cost a great deal.

Giving up is withdrawal of attention. Caring less, done properly, is the opposite — it takes more attention, because you have to watch someone make a choice you wouldn't make and stay warm anyway. Self-determination theory calls the alternative autonomy support: staying involved, offering information and help, and leaving the decision genuinely with the person. It is consistently associated with better long-term maintenance of a behaviour than pressure is, because the reason for doing it ends up belonging to them.

This matters. Caring less is a framework for preferences and habits, not for safety. Signs of genuine cognitive decline, falls, malnutrition, untreated symptoms, unmanaged medication or any sudden change in how someone is functioning are not situations to release — they are situations to involve a doctor in, promptly. Backing off from managing someone's exercise routine is very different from backing off when something is actually wrong, and knowing the difference is part of caring well.

Expect the worry to outlast the behaviour. Stopping the reminders is a decision you can make in a day; the anxiety that used to discharge itself through those reminders takes considerably longer to settle, and the first few weeks often feel worse rather than better because nothing is absorbing it. It helps to give the impulse somewhere else to go — a walk, a note in a journal, a message to a friend instead of to the person — and to treat the discomfort as withdrawal rather than as evidence you were right to be managing things. Reclaiming that attention is its own project, and slow living in a fast world is the wider version of it.

Then you have lost nothing you actually had, because the version of them you were trying to produce was never available to you in the first place. This is the part worth being honest about: caring less is not a technique for getting a different person. It is a way of no longer making your own peace conditional on their choices. Sometimes the behaviour shifts once the pressure lifts. Sometimes it doesn't, and what changes instead is how much of your day it occupies.
