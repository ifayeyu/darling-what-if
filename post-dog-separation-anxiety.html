What If Your Dog Isn't Being Dramatic —
They're Panicking?

August 15, 2026
By Faye Livia
12 min read

You set up the camera expecting to feel reassured. What you watch instead is twenty minutes of something you have no comfortable word for.

Most people find out by accident. A neighbour mentions, kindly, that there's been some howling. Or you set up a cheap camera out of mild curiosity, expecting to watch your dog sleep on the sofa she isn't allowed on, and instead you watch her stand at the door and not move for eleven minutes.

Then she starts to pace. Then she starts to drool.

And the thing that undoes people — the reason this is one of the hardest things to watch in the whole of dog ownership — is that she isn't doing anything wrong. There's nothing to tell her off for. She is just very frightened, alone, in a room, and she has been for some time, and you had no idea.

The short answer

Separation anxiety is a panic response, not a behaviour problem. The chewing, howling and accidents are symptoms of that panic — never spite, never protest. And because a panicking animal cannot learn, discipline doesn't just fail here. It actively makes it worse.

Why Everyone Started Searching for This at Once

This became a globally dominant search term in a very short window, and the reason is not mysterious.

An enormous number of dogs were acquired during 2020 and 2021 — the Royal Veterinary College's research on what they termed "pandemic puppies" documented both the scale of that boom and the behavioural fallout that followed. Those dogs did something no previous generation of dogs had done at that scale: they spent their entire critical socialisation window in houses where a human was always there.

Nobody was doing anything wrong. It simply never came up. Ordinarily a puppy learns absence the way children learn most things — accidentally, in small doses, constantly. Someone pops to the shop. Someone showers. Someone goes to work and comes back, and comes back, and comes back, until the coming back is so predictable it stops being a question.

Those dogs never got the small doses. Then in 2021 and 2022 the world reopened, and a great many of them experienced genuine solitude for the first time as adults, with no preparation and no evidence that it ends.

20–30 min
When the distress peaks. Separation anxiety is overwhelmingly a problem of the first half hour. Dogs who are going to panic almost always do it early, then either exhaust themselves into a fitful rest or continue at a lower intensity. This is why camera footage is so much more useful than the state of the flat when you get home — the evidence you need is in the first twenty minutes, not the last.

What the Footage Actually Shows

If you've never watched it, the sequence is remarkably consistent — and knowing it makes the difference between recognising a panic disorder and thinking you own a badly behaved dog.

01

It starts before you leave

The keys. The particular shoes. The laptop bag, the coat, the sound of the specific cupboard. Watch closely and you'll often see the dog's body change while you're still in the house — a stillness, a following, an anxious watching. By the time the door shuts, they have already been anxious for ten minutes.

02

The door vigil

Most dogs go straight to the exit and stay there. Not scratching yet — just standing, or sitting, facing it. People find this the hardest part of the footage to watch, and it's the clearest signal that this isn't boredom. A bored dog goes looking for something to do. An anxious dog stays at the place you disappeared.

03

Physical stress you can see

Panting with no heat and no exercise. Drooling — sometimes enough to leave a wet patch. Pacing the same route. Whale eye, flattened ears, a lowered tail. These are autonomic responses. They are not choices, and they cannot be trained away with correction.

04

The escape attempt

Destruction in separation anxiety is nearly always directed at exit points — doorframes, the area under the door, window sills, the hallway. It is not random mischief. It is an animal trying to get out and find you, and it's where the most serious self-injury happens.

05

Untouched food

This one is diagnostic and widely underrated. Most dogs will not eat when genuinely frightened. If the stuffed food toy you left is exactly where you put it, that tells you more than any amount of damage does.

She did not destroy the door because she was angry that you left. She destroyed the door because she was trying to follow you, and the door was in the way.

Three Things It Isn't

Most of the failed advice out there comes from misidentifying the problem in one of three ways.

What people assume

  • "She's bored" — so they buy more toys and walk her harder
  • "She's punishing me" — so they come home cold, or tell her off
  • "She's lonely" — so they get a second dog
  • "She'll grow out of it" — so they wait

What's actually true

  • Boredom starts later and targets objects, not doorways, and comes without physical stress signs
  • Dogs don't do revenge. It needs a theory of mind nobody has demonstrated in dogs — the same misreading behind the "guilty look"
  • It's usually hyper-attachment to one person, so another dog often doesn't help — and may learn the panic
  • Untreated, it typically entrenches, because every terrifying absence confirms the fear

The Cues You Don't Know You're Giving

Here's the part that changes things fastest, and costs nothing.

Your dog has built a precise map of what precedes your absence. Not just the keys — the order of the keys. The shower at a particular time. Putting the laptop in the bag. Closing the bedroom door on the way past. Each of those is a small alarm bell, and by the time you actually leave, the dog has been climbing for twenty minutes.

The work is to make those cues stop meaning anything. Pick up your keys and sit back down. Put your shoes on and make a cup of tea. Get your coat, walk to the door, hang the coat back up. Do it until the cue is boring — which takes days, not minutes, and feels faintly ridiculous while you're doing it.

The work that actually helps
  • Get a camera before anything else. You cannot treat what you cannot see, and you need to know where the threshold is — the point at which distress begins. Everything else depends on that number.
  • Start below the threshold, even if it's seconds. If she panics at ninety seconds, the training absence is sixty. Not five minutes because five minutes seems short to you. The whole method rests on the dog never actually panicking during practice.
  • Decouple the departure cues. Keys, shoes, bag, coat — perform them at random times, going nowhere, until they stop predicting anything.
  • Keep arrivals and departures dull. No emotional goodbye, no ecstatic reunion. Both raise the emotional stakes of the door. Greet her calmly a few minutes after you're back, once she's settled.
  • Build independence while you're home. If she follows you room to room and lies pressed against you constantly, gently practise being in different rooms. A dog who has never been alone while you're home has no practice at all at being alone.
  • Use sniffing, not sprinting. Scent work and food puzzles genuinely lower arousal. A hard run before you leave can leave a dog over-aroused rather than settled.
  • Increase absences unevenly. Ten minutes, then three, then twelve, then five. Predictable escalation teaches the dog that every absence is longer than the last, which is the opposite of what you want.
  • Arrange cover while you train. Sitter, daycare, a neighbour, working from home where possible. Every full-blown panic during the training period sets you back, so the goal is that she is not left alone beyond her threshold at all for a while.

What Makes It Measurably Worse

Some of the most commonly given advice is actively harmful here, and it's worth being blunt about which.

Punishment on your return. The dog cannot connect your anger to something that happened two hours ago. What she learns instead is that your homecoming is unpredictable and frightening — which raises baseline anxiety and makes the next absence worse. This is the mechanism I wrote about in the guilty look piece, and separation anxiety is where it does the most damage.

Confining a panicking dog. If a dog is already relaxed in a crate, it can be a genuine comfort. If she isn't, containment during panic is how dogs break teeth, tear nails and injure their faces trying to get out. Containment is not treatment.

"Flooding" — leaving her to cry it out. The theory is that she'll learn it's fine. What actually happens is repeated, prolonged exposure to terror with no escape, which entrenches the fear rather than extinguishing it. There is broad consensus in veterinary behaviour against this approach.

Waiting. Separation anxiety does not usually resolve on its own, because every unsupervised absence is another rehearsal of the panic.

You are not training her to tolerate being alone. You are collecting evidence, absence by absence, that you always come back — and she is the one who has to be convinced.

When to bring in help

This is one of the clearest cases in dog ownership for getting professional support early rather than late. Please speak to your vet if:

  • Your dog is injuring herself — bleeding paws or mouth, broken nails, damaged teeth
  • She cannot be left even briefly without full-blown panic
  • There's no improvement after several weeks of consistent, careful work
  • The distress is escalating, or spilling into times when you are home
  • You are exhausted, dreading leaving the house, or grieving your own freedom — which is extremely common and rarely said out loud

Ask your vet for a referral to a qualified, force-free clinical animal behaviourist. A vet visit is also worth it to rule out pain or medical causes, which can masquerade as or worsen anxiety. Severe cases are managed with a structured plan drawn up by professionals — that plan is theirs to design, not something to assemble from an article.

The Honest Timeline

Weeks to months. Not days. And not in a straight line — there will be a fortnight of real progress followed by a bad Tuesday for no discoverable reason, and that is normal rather than evidence that it isn't working.

The most common reason this fails is going too fast. It is genuinely hard to sit in a car around the corner adding fifteen seconds a session when what you want is to be able to go to dinner. But every absence that tips her into panic undoes several that didn't, so slow is not the cautious option here. Slow is the fast option.

She isn't giving you a hard time. She's having one.

Somewhere in the middle of all this it stops being a training problem and becomes something simpler and harder: an animal who loves you past the point of comfort, and doesn't yet know that leaving isn't the same as gone. You can't explain it to her. You can only show her, in increments so small they feel absurd, that the door opens again. It does take months. It also works — and there are few better feelings than watching the footage one ordinary afternoon and finding she slept through the whole thing.

Questions About Separation Anxiety

It's a panic response, not a behaviour problem. When a dog with separation anxiety is left alone, they experience genuine distress on the level of a panic attack — elevated heart rate, stress hormones, an inability to settle or eat. The destruction, howling and toileting that owners find afterwards are symptoms of that panic, not acts of protest or revenge. This distinction matters enormously, because a dog in a panic state cannot learn, which is why discipline-based approaches consistently fail and often make things worse.

The clearest tells are timing and location. Separation anxiety usually begins within the first fifteen to thirty minutes of you leaving and often before you have even gone — at the sight of keys or shoes. Damage clusters at exit points: doors, window frames, the hallway. You may also see drooling, panting, pacing, or house-soiling in a dog who is otherwise reliably clean. Boredom, by contrast, tends to start later into the absence, targets interesting objects rather than doorways, and does not come with the physiological signs of distress. A cheap indoor camera settles the question faster than any checklist.

Usually not, and it is one of the more expensive mistakes people make. True separation anxiety is typically hyper-attachment to a specific person rather than a general dislike of solitude, so another dog in the room does not resolve the absence that matters. There is also a real risk the second dog learns the distress behaviour by observation. Some dogs do have isolation distress, which is a related but distinct problem where any company helps — but that should be established with a professional rather than assumed.

It helps a little and it is not a solution. A physically tired dog may take slightly longer to become distressed, but exercise does not address the underlying panic about being alone — and an over-exercised, over-aroused dog can actually be harder to settle. Gentle enrichment such as sniffing or a food puzzle before departure is more useful than a hard run, because sniffing is genuinely calming for dogs in a way that cardio is not.

Meaningful progress is usually measured in weeks to months rather than days, and setbacks are a normal part of the process rather than a sign of failure. The single most common reason treatment fails is moving too fast — increasing absence time faster than the dog can tolerate, which reinforces the fear rather than reducing it. Progress that feels almost boringly slow is progress that lasts.

A dog in a panic state who is also confined can injure themselves badly — broken teeth, torn nails and facial injuries from attempting to escape are well documented. For some dogs a crate is a safe den and helps; for a panicking dog it can escalate distress considerably. This is exactly the kind of decision worth making with a qualified behaviourist rather than alone.
