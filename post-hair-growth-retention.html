What If Growing Your Hair Has Nothing
to Do With Growing It?

August 15, 2026
By Faye Livia
12 min read

There is a ponytail that has been exactly the same length for two years. You have tried the vitamins and the expensive shampoo. The problem was never the growing.

Here is something I only understood properly after years of buying the wrong things: my hair had never once stopped growing. Not for a single month. It was growing the entire time I was standing in front of the mirror wondering why it wouldn't.

It just wasn't staying.

That distinction sounds small and it changes everything, because almost the whole beauty industry is built on the first idea — grow it faster, grow it thicker, grow it now — and almost none of it is built on the second one, which is the one that actually determines the length of your hair.

The short answer

You cannot meaningfully speed hair growth up. It grows around 1cm — roughly half an inch — a month, for almost everyone, almost regardless of what you do. Length is growth minus breakage. The breakage is the part you control.

The Number That Reframes Everything

Scalp hair grows at a remarkably consistent rate. Roughly a centimetre a month, about six inches a year, with small variation by genetics, age and general health. Illness slows it. Ageing slows it a little. Almost nothing else moves it much at all.

Which means that if your hair has been shoulder-length for three years, you have grown something like eighteen inches of hair in that time. Eighteen inches that arrived, travelled down the shaft, and then broke off somewhere along the way without you ever noticing it happen.

~1cm
Per month. That's the ceiling, and you already have it. No serum, supplement or scalp treatment has been shown to raise that rate meaningfully in healthy people. The variable that differs enormously between two women with the same growth rate and wildly different hair lengths is not production. It's survival.

Your Hair Is Not Always Growing

The second thing worth knowing is that at any given moment, a meaningful proportion of your hair isn't growing at all — and that's completely normal. Every follicle runs its own independent cycle, which is why we don't moult in season like a dog does.

01

Anagen — the growing years

The active phase, and the one that sets your maximum length. It runs for roughly two to six years, and that range is largely genetic. Someone whose hair reaches their waist isn't growing faster than you; they're growing for longer before the follicle resets. Around 85–90% of your hair is here right now.

02

Catagen — the brief goodbye

A short transition of two or three weeks in which the follicle detaches from its blood supply and growth stops. Only a tiny fraction of hair is in this phase at once.

03

Telogen — the resting phase

Around three months of nothing at all. The hair sits in place, fully formed, no longer growing, waiting. Roughly 10–15% of your hair is resting at any moment. Remember this three-month figure — it explains almost everything about unexpected shedding.

04

Exogen — the release

The resting hair falls, and a new one is already on its way up beneath it. Losing 50 to 100 hairs a day is entirely normal. The hair in your brush is usually evidence of a system working, not failing.

Your hair is not a plant you can fertilise into growing faster. It's a crop you're already harvesting — and mostly losing before you get it home.

Why Shedding Always Arrives Three Months Late

This is the part I wish someone had told me a decade ago, because it makes sense of an experience almost every woman has had and almost nobody diagnoses correctly.

When the body goes through something significant, it makes a triage decision. Hair is, biologically speaking, optional. So a large number of follicles get pushed out of their growing phase early and into that resting phase — and then, because telogen lasts about three months, those hairs sit there quietly for twelve weeks before they let go.

Then they all release at roughly the same time. You stand in the shower in April, horrified, and search for what you might have done wrong in April. The answer is in January.

What you blame

  • The new shampoo — you switched a few weeks ago, so it must be that
  • Your hairdresser — something they did last month
  • Hard water, the season, your age
  • Something being deeply wrong — because the volume is genuinely frightening

What actually happened, twelve weeks ago

  • A stretch of real stress — a bereavement, a job loss, a move, a crisis
  • Illness with a high fever, or surgery, or a severe infection
  • Rapid weight loss or under-eating — especially low protein
  • Childbirth — the classic and most predictable trigger of all
  • Low iron, which builds quietly over months

Dermatologists call this telogen effluvium, and the most important thing about it is also the most reassuring: it is usually self-limiting. The follicles are not damaged. They have paused. Once the trigger has passed, they restart, and the hair comes back — though it takes months, and the regrowth arrives as short wispy pieces around your hairline that look like flyaways and are actually good news.

The hair you are losing this month is a message from the woman you were three months ago. Usually she was going through something. Usually she was doing her best.

The Things Quietly Costing You Length

If growth is fixed and shedding is mostly downstream of your life, then the lever you actually hold is breakage. This is unglamorous and it works.

Where the length is going
  • Heat, used daily. Repeated high heat degrades the cuticle — the overlapping outer layer that protects each strand. Lower the temperature, use a heat protectant, and let it be air-dried more often than not. Every skipped blow-dry is length banked.
  • Brushing hair when it's soaking wet. Wet hair stretches, and stretched hair snaps. Let it dry to damp, then work upward from the ends with a wide-tooth comb — never down from the roots, which drags every tangle into a knot at the bottom.
  • Bleach and heavy lightening. Chemically lifting colour opens the cuticle to remove pigment, and it doesn't fully close again. Staying within a few shades of your natural colour is far gentler than going dramatically lighter.
  • Tight styles, worn constantly. Tension at the follicle is the one form of damage that can eventually become permanent. If a style hurts, or you feel it when you raise your eyebrows, it is too tight. Vary where the tension sits.
  • Cotton pillowcases. Cotton grips hair and creates friction across seven or eight hours of movement every night. Silk or satin — a pillowcase or a bonnet — is one of the few genuinely effortless changes on this list.
  • Washing more than your scalp needs. Not a moral position, just mechanics: every wash is another round of swelling, friction and manipulation. Find your actual frequency rather than a default one.
  • Skipping conditioner, or only using it at the roots. The ends of long hair are years old. They have earned the most protection and usually get the least.
  • Never trimming. A split doesn't stay where it starts — it travels up the shaft and the strand breaks higher than the original damage. Losing a quarter inch deliberately beats losing two inches by accident.

The Natural Rituals Actually Worth Your Time

I have tried an embarrassing number of things. These are the ones I still do.

Scalp massage. A few minutes with your fingertips, most days. The evidence is real but modest — small studies suggest the mechanical stretching of cells in the scalp may influence hair thickness, which is a more interesting mechanism than the "boosting circulation" explanation usually given. It costs nothing and it is lovely, which is reason enough. I've written more about treating your scalp as skin and why that shift matters.

Pre-wash oiling. An hour or so with oil worked through the mid-lengths and ends before shampooing. This isn't about feeding the hair — hair is not alive and cannot be nourished from outside. It reduces how much water the shaft absorbs during washing, and less swelling means less stress on the cuticle. Coconut oil has the best evidence here because of how its molecule penetrates the shaft.

Rosemary. Worth mentioning honestly rather than breathlessly. There is a small body of trial evidence suggesting rosemary oil, applied diluted to the scalp over months, may help — but the studies are few, small, and take at least six months to show anything. It is a reasonable ritual and not a miracle, and it must be diluted in a carrier oil.

Protective looseness. Not elaborate protective styling, just the habit of keeping hair contained and untugged when you sleep, exercise or spend a windy day outside. A loose braid does more for your ends over a year than most products.

Feeding It From the Inside

Hair is made almost entirely of protein, and it is the first thing the body deprioritises when something is short. So nutrition matters — but not in the way the supplement aisle suggests.

The honest position is this: correcting a real deficiency helps; adding more on top of sufficiency does not. Iron, protein, zinc, vitamin D and the B vitamins are the ones repeatedly linked to hair health, and low ferritin in particular is a common, quiet and frequently missed cause of shedding in women. That is a blood test, not a guess. There is no supplement demonstrated to make hair grow faster than its natural rate, and taking high doses of some — biotin especially — can interfere with thyroid and cardiac blood tests, which is worth telling your doctor about.

The food-first version of all this I've written out properly in the fourteen foods your hair actually wants, and it pairs with what's happening lower down — the gut-skin axis shapes how much of any of it you absorb in the first place.

When it's worth seeing someone

Most shedding is temporary and resolves on its own. Please do get it looked at properly if you notice:

  • Sudden, heavy loss, or a parting that is visibly widening
  • Distinct bald patches, or scalp visible through the hair
  • Redness, scaling, pain, burning or persistent itching
  • Shedding alongside fatigue, weight change, or changes to your cycle
  • Heavy shedding that continues past about six months

These point to something a GP or dermatologist should assess rather than something to manage with a ritual — including anything you read here.

The Honest Timeline

Nothing here works quickly, and I would rather say that plainly than sell you a month.

If you stop breaking your hair today, you will not see it for a season. Hair grows six inches a year, so the difference between a year of careful handling and a year of careless handling is measured in inches — real, visible inches, but only in hindsight. If you are recovering from a shedding episode, expect three to six months before regrowth is obvious, and closer to a year before it blends in.

The reframe that helped me was giving up on the idea of a hair routine that does something and replacing it with one that stops doing damage. The first is exciting and mostly futile. The second is boring and it is the entire game.

You are already growing all the hair you need. The work is letting it stay.

Half an inch a month is arriving whether you buy anything or not. It has been arriving this whole time. So the question was never how to make more of it — it was what has been happening to it between the scalp and the mirror. Turn the heat down. Let it dry before you touch it. Sleep on something smoother. Eat enough, actually enough. And be gentle with yourself about the shedding, because it is usually your body telling you that three months ago was harder than you let on.

On the growth cycle and shedding — the anagen/catagen/telogen framework and the roughly three-month delay in telogen effluvium are standard dermatology, set out clearly by the American Academy of Dermatology and the Cleveland Clinic. Harvard Health covers the stress mechanism.

On nutrition — the review most worth reading is "Diet and hair loss: effects of nutrient deficiency and supplement use" (Dermatology Practical & Conceptual), which is where the correct-a-deficiency-but-don't-over-supplement position comes from. Biotin's interference with thyroid and troponin blood tests is an FDA safety communication.

On scalp massage — the mechanism described comes from "Standardized Scalp Massage Results in Increased Hair Thickness" (Eplasty, 2016), with follow-up survey data in Dermatology and Therapy (2019). Both are small studies; I've tried to describe them at the weight they deserve rather than more.

Two things flagged as weaker evidence: the rosemary oil trial base is small and short, and the coconut oil penetration finding, while real, rests on a limited number of studies. Neither is a reason not to try them — both are a reason not to expect much, quickly.

If your hair is shedding heavily, your parting is widening, or anything here sounds like your situation, please see a GP or dermatologist rather than working from an article — including this one.

Questions About Hair Growth

Not meaningfully, no. Scalp hair grows at a fairly fixed rate of roughly 1cm — about half an inch — per month, and that rate is set largely by genetics, age and overall health. No shampoo, serum or supplement has been shown to override it. What varies enormously between people is not how fast hair grows but how much of that growth survives. Length is what remains after breakage, and that part is very much within your control.

Because it is breaking at roughly the same rate it is growing. Every strand also has a genetically determined maximum growing phase — for most people somewhere between two and six years — which sets a natural terminal length. If your hair reaches your shoulders and stalls, the usual explanation is that the ends are wearing out and snapping before they can get further, not that growth has switched off.

This is the most confusing thing about hair, and the reason so few people connect cause and effect. A significant physical or emotional stressor can push a large number of follicles out of their growing phase and into a resting phase early. Those hairs stay in place for roughly three months before they release. So the shedding you notice in April often belongs to something that happened in January. Dermatologists call this telogen effluvium, and in most cases it is self-limiting and resolves once the trigger has passed.

The proposed mechanism is mechanical rather than circulatory in the way people usually imagine — stretching forces applied to cells in the scalp appear to influence hair thickness. It is genuinely worth doing because it is free, pleasant and carries no downside, but it is a small lever rather than a transformation.

Trimming does not affect growth at all — that happens at the follicle, not the ends. But it does protect length, which is why the myth persists and why the advice is still good. A split end does not stay put; it travels upward along the shaft and the strand eventually snaps higher than where the damage began. Removing a small amount regularly means you lose a quarter inch on purpose instead of two inches by accident.
