What Your Screen Is Quietly
Doing to Your Skin

Updated November 24, 2026
By Faye Livia
7 min read

You've probably heard that your laptop is quietly aging your face. The research tells a calmer story — and points to what actually deserves your attention.

No one could have anticipated how completely the screen would become our primary environment. Meetings, celebrations, social hours, creative work, rest — all of it happening through a rectangle of light, inches from our faces, for hours at a time.

So it makes sense that "blue light skincare" became a category almost overnight: serums, mists and SPFs promising to shield your face from your laptop. I wanted to know whether the worry holds up. The answer turned out to be more reassuring than the marketing — and more useful, because it points to a different culprit entirely.

What blue light actually is

Blue light, sometimes called high-energy visible (HEV) light, is the short-wavelength end of the light you can see, roughly 400 to 500 nanometres. It sits right next to ultraviolet on the spectrum, which is why it gets talked about in the same breath as UV. Screens, LED bulbs and phones all give off some of it.

But by far the biggest source of blue light in your life is the sun. Visible light makes up around half of the sunlight that reaches us, and that is the blue light skin researchers are actually worried about.

The number that changes the conversation

When researchers at Beiersdorf (the company behind Nivea) measured how much blue light everyday devices actually give off, the comparison was striking: a full week sitting uninterrupted 30 cm from a monitor delivered about the same blue light dose as one minute outdoors at midday on a sunny summer day in Hamburg. A ten-hour phone call worked out about the same.

It's fair to note that a sunscreen company ran that study. But an independent clinical study points the same way. In 2020, dermatologists in Nice exposed women with melasma — a condition that is unusually sensitive to visible light — to screen-level blue light for eight hours a day, five days in a row, at just 20 cm. Their pigmentation did not get worse.

Where visible light does matter: the sun

None of this means blue light is harmless. It means the dose matters, and the dose from the sun is in a different league.

In lab and clinical studies, visible light from sunlight (or from solar simulators) increases free radicals in the skin and can trigger pigmentation. In medium and darker skin tones, that pigmentation tends to be darker and to last longer than a tan from UV alone. It is also one reason melasma and post-inflammatory dark spots keep coming back even in people who wear sunscreen faithfully.

The catch is that most sunscreens aren't designed to block visible light. Standard broad-spectrum filters cover UVA and UVB. To block visible light, a sunscreen has to be visible on the skin — which is why the products that do this are tinted, usually with iron oxides. In randomized trials, people with melasma who used an iron-oxide tinted sunscreen saw better results than those using an untinted one.

If you're noticing

Dark spots or melasma that keep coming back

This is the one situation where visible light genuinely deserves your attention — and the fix isn't a blue light serum. It's switching to a tinted mineral sunscreen with iron oxides, worn every day, including the days you sit near a bright window. If you have darker skin or a history of melasma, it's worth asking a dermatologist about too.

If you're noticing

Dullness and fine lines

Look at sun and sleep before screens. Daily broad-spectrum SPF remains the best-evidenced way to slow visible aging, and a morning antioxidant like vitamin C helps mop up the free radicals that sunlight generates. Retinoids at night are still the most reliable ingredient for texture and fine lines. (I wrote about the order to layer them in here.)

If you're noticing

Breakouts along your cheek and jaw

If there's a screen connection here, it's more likely to be contact than light: a phone pressed to your cheek, or a chin resting in your hand through long calls. Wiping your phone screen regularly and using headphones for long calls costs nothing and rules it out. Resist the urge to scrub harder — over-exfoliating usually makes congestion worse, not better.

"Your laptop probably isn't aging your skin. The bright window beside it might be."

What screens do change

The real effect of screens on skin is indirect. Glass blocks most UVB but lets much of the UVA and visible light through, so a desk beside a sunny window can add up to meaningful exposure over a working year — far more than the monitor in front of you.

Then there's sleep. Blue light in the evening reaches your eyes, not your skin, and it can delay melatonin and push bedtime later. Short, poor sleep shows up on the face: in one study, chronically poor sleepers had more signs of skin aging and their skin recovered more slowly after sun exposure and barrier disruption. Putting the phone down earlier is probably the most underrated screen-related thing you can do for your skin. (More on that in my evening routine for better sleep.)

What I'd actually do

Skip paying extra for "blue light protection" claims — there's no standardised test behind them, so the label doesn't tell you much. Put the money and attention into the few things with real evidence:

A simple daily sequence

AM Gentle cleanse → vitamin C serum → moisturiser → broad-spectrum SPF 30+ (tinted with iron oxides if you get dark spots or melasma)
PM Cleanse (double cleanse if you wore SPF) → retinoid 2–3 nights a week → moisturiser
Day Notice where the sun hits your desk. Reapply SPF if you sit in direct window light for hours.
Night Dim screens and use night mode for the last hour — for your sleep, not your skin.

If sunscreen is the step you keep skipping, start there — this is how I finally made it stick. Everything else in a routine is built on top of it.

Your skin is paying attention to your environment, even when you're not. The good news is that the part of that environment worth worrying about is mostly outside your window, not on your desk.
