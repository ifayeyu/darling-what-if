What If the Best Trip of Your Life
Was One You Took Alone?

September 12, 2026
By Faye Livia
9 min read

There is a particular kind of quiet that arrives when you step off a plane in a country where no one knows your name — and no one is waiting for you. It can feel like fear. It can also feel, once you let it, like the freest you’ve ever been. This is the case for going anyway.

My first solo trip was Paris — and I will love it forever for the most gloriously unglamorous reason. I ate pastry for three meals a day. A warm croissant at dawn, something buttery and impossible at noon, a slice of tart before bed — and not one person to raise an eyebrow, nag me, or mention the word calories. I walked the whole city instead, all day, every day, arrondissement to arrondissement, working off the éclairs entirely by accident and stopping wherever I pleased. No itinerary to negotiate. No one to please. No judgement. Just me, a paper bag from the boulangerie, and a freedom so complete it made me laugh out loud on a bridge over the Seine. I had braced for loneliness. What I found instead was pure fun — and something I can only describe as coming home to myself.

If you’ve been quietly wondering whether to do it — to book the trip, alone, on purpose — let me be the experienced friend who tells you the truth about all of it: why it changes you, whether it’s really safe, and exactly how to begin.

You’re in Better Company Than You Think

First, dispel the image of the solo female traveler as some rare, reckless outlier. She is, statistically, the main character of modern travel.

75–84%
of the world’s solo travelers are now women — and Google searches for “solo female travel” have risen more than eleven-fold since 2011. Traveling alone as a woman isn’t an anomaly anymore. It’s a movement.

Women aren’t just participating in solo travel; they’re driving it. Which means that wherever you go, you are joining a vast, quiet sisterhood of women who booked the flight, felt the fear, and went anyway — and came back different.

Why It Changes You

Traveling with people you love is a joy. But it also lets you outsource things — the navigating, the deciding, the small brave acts of asking a stranger for help. Travel alone and there is no one to outsource to. Every problem is yours to solve, which means every solved problem becomes proof, banked quietly inside you: I can do this. I did do this.

That’s the real souvenir. Not the photos, but the self-trust. You learn that you can read a train timetable in a language you don’t speak, sit alone at dinner without shrinking, change your mind with no one to consult, and be excellent company for yourself. You come home with a spine a little straighter — and a quiet certainty that the person you can most rely on is you.

You go looking for a place. You come back having found a person — yourself, met on your own terms for the first time.

The Quiet Luxury of Romancing Yourself

There is a particular magic to visiting a beautiful, romantic city entirely alone — and it surprises people, because we’re taught that romance requires someone else. It doesn’t. Some of the most tender days of my life have been ones I spent as my own companion.

Total freedom. You set the pace and answer to no one. Linger three hours in front of a single painting or leave after ten minutes. Eat wherever you want, whenever you’re hungry, with no compromise and no committee. The whole day bends to exactly one person’s wishes — yours.

Romancing yourself. Take yourself to the beautiful restaurant and order the good wine. Walk to the viewpoint for sunset and watch it without narrating it to anyone. When you stop waiting to share beauty and simply let yourself receive it, something shifts: you become the one who shows up for you, who treats you well, who decides you’re worth the fine dining and the scenic detour. It is the most underrated love affair there is.

But Is It Safe?

Let’s meet the question everyone’s really asking, honestly — because reassurance that ignores the fear is useless. Yes, solo female travel carries risk. So does driving to work. The point is not fearlessness; it’s preparation. The safest solo travelers are not the boldest — they’re the most quietly deliberate.

The overwhelming majority of women who travel alone come home with nothing worse than a great story and slightly emptier savings. You tilt the odds further in your favour with a handful of simple habits: research your neighbourhood before you book, arrive somewhere new in daylight, keep someone at home updated on your plans, hold your valuables close, and — above all — treat your intuition as data. If something feels off, you are allowed to leave. No politeness is worth overriding that quiet inner alarm.

How to Take Your First Solo Trip

The leap feels enormous from the sofa and surprisingly small once you’ve booked. Here’s how to make the first one gentle.

Start easy, not epic. Choose a first destination that’s safe, walkable, and used to solo visitors. Save the ambitious, off-grid dream for trip three.

Book the first two nights — then breathe. You don’t need every night planned. Lock in a safe, central place to land, and let the rest of the trip stay open.

Arrive in daylight. The single easiest safety upgrade there is. A new place is far less daunting — and far easier to read — in the light.

Stay central and social. A well-reviewed hostel, guesthouse, or small hotel in a lively area gives you both safety in numbers and easy company whenever you want it.

Share your itinerary. Send someone at home your rough plan and check in on a loose rhythm. Independence and a safety net are not opposites.

Eat at the bar. Dining alone feels less exposed at the counter — and it’s where you’ll fall into the easiest conversations of the trip.

Trust the gut, every time. Your instincts are not paranoia; they’re millennia of evolution. If a street, a person, or a plan feels wrong, honour it without explanation.

Where to Go First

If you want a destination that holds your hand a little on the first trip, start here.

Japan

Famously safe, spotlessly clean, and astonishingly easy to navigate — even without the language. Solo dining is completely normal, and the trains run like clockwork. The gentlest possible plunge into the deep end.

Portugal

Warm in every sense — affordable, walkable, sun-soaked, and full of other solo travelers. Lisbon and Porto are welcoming and easy, with a café-and-coastline pace that suits a first trip beautifully.

New Zealand

Exceptionally safe, English-speaking, and impossibly scenic, with a backpacker infrastructure built for exactly this. Ideal if you want nature, space, and easy conversation.

Iceland

Consistently ranked among the safest countries on earth, with otherworldly landscapes and a compact, easy loop. A dramatic backdrop for a quietly life-changing first solo week.

The trip is never really about the place.

It’s about who you become when you finally stop waiting for someone to come with you. You don’t need a companion, or permission, or the perfect moment — you need a safe first destination, a booked flight, and the willingness to be a little brave. Somewhere out there is a version of you who already went, and came back luminous. Go and meet her. She’s been waiting.
