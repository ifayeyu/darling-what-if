Vitamin C for Skin: Why Eating It May Beat Your Serum

November 24, 2026
By Faye Livia
9 min read

For a decade we've been told vitamin C belongs in a small dark bottle. A landmark study has just demonstrated that the version your skin responds to most dramatically arrives a different way entirely — through your bloodstream.

There is a bottle on almost every bathroom shelf I've ever been shown. Amber glass, usually. A dropper. Somewhere between thirty and two hundred pounds, depending on how persuasive the packaging was. Vitamin C serum is the single most recommended product in modern skincare — by dermatologists, by facialists, by every beauty editor I know, myself included.

None of that is wrong. But a study published in the Journal of Investigative Dermatology in late 2025 has quietly rearranged the furniture, and the finding is the kind that makes you look at your serum differently — not with disappointment, exactly. More like the feeling of realising you'd been reading only one page of a two-page letter.

Researchers at the University of Otago in Christchurch, working with collaborators in Germany, asked a deceptively simple question: when you eat vitamin C, where does it actually go?

Two Kiwifruit a Day

The design was elegant in its modesty. Around two dozen healthy adults, split across New Zealand and Germany. No serums, no supplements, no lasers, no needles. Each participant ate two vitamin C–rich SunGold kiwifruit every day for eight weeks. Kiwifruit was chosen for an unglamorous reason: its vitamin C content is consistent and easy to measure.

Before and after, researchers took actual skin samples — not questionnaires about how glowy people felt, but tissue. The basal layers were analysed in Christchurch; the outer dermal layers and skin function tests were handled in Germany. Ultrasound measured thickness.

What they found is the part worth sitting with.

Every layer
Vitamin C from food travelled through the bloodstream into all layers of the skin, where it increased skin thickness — a direct marker of collagen production in the dermis — and drove measurable renewal of epidermal cells. Skin vitamin C levels tracked blood levels so closely that lead researcher Professor Margreet Vissers described the correlation as far more marked than in any other organ her team has studied (Journal of Investigative Dermatology, 2025).

Read that again, because the beauty industry has spent fifteen years telling you the opposite story. The skin doesn't merely tolerate vitamin C delivered from the inside. It appears to prioritise it — absorbing it from circulation more eagerly than almost any other tissue in the body, and distributing it all the way down to the layer where collagen is actually built.

Your serum has to negotiate with a barrier that evolved specifically to keep things out. Your bloodstream has a key to every door.

Why Vitamin C and Collagen Are Inseparable

This isn't a marketing association. It's chemistry, and it's non-negotiable.

Collagen isn't simply extruded by your cells in finished form. It has to be built and then stabilised, and two enzymes do that stabilising: prolyl hydroxylase and lysyl hydroxylase. Both of them require vitamin C as a cofactor. Not "benefit from." Require. Without it, those enzymes can't complete the job, and the collagen your body produces is structurally weaker.

The historical proof of this is brutal and completely convincing. Scurvy — vitamin C deficiency — didn't only cause bleeding gums. It caused old, long-healed wounds to reopen, because the collagen holding those scars together could no longer be maintained. Sailors watched injuries from years earlier come undone. That is what happens to human structural tissue when this one molecule runs out.

So when a serum promises firmness, it isn't lying about the mechanism. It's just working on the topmost floors of a building whose foundations are poured somewhere else entirely. If you want the fuller picture of how collagen is actually made and maintained, I've written about whether collagen supplements genuinely work separately.

What Else It Does Besides Firmness

Collagen is the headline, but it isn't the whole reason vitamin C has held its position at the top of every dermatologist's list for twenty years.

01

Brightening and a more even tone

Vitamin C interferes with tyrosinase, the enzyme that drives melanin production. That's the mechanism behind its reputation for brightening dull skin and gradually softening the look of dark spots and post-inflammatory marks — the shadows left behind after a spot has healed. It won't erase pigmentation overnight, but consistent use is one of the gentlest routes to a more even skin tone.

02

Antioxidant defence where the damage happens

UV exposure and pollution generate free radicals at the skin's surface, and that oxidative stress degrades existing collagen faster than you can rebuild it. A topical antioxidant sits exactly where that damage occurs. This is the one job where a serum genuinely outperforms your dinner plate.

03

It makes your SPF work harder

Vitamin C is not sunscreen and will never replace it. But antioxidants and SPF address different halves of the same problem — sunscreen limits how much UV reaches the skin, while vitamin C helps neutralise the free radicals produced by whatever gets through. Layered under your daily SPF, the pairing is more protective than either alone.

So Should You Bin the Serum?

No. And I want to be precise here, because the internet is already flattening this study into "skincare is a scam," which it absolutely does not say.

Topical vitamin C has its own solid evidence base. Research published in Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology found topical vitamin C increased collagen synthesis and improved firmness over twelve weeks. It also delivers something eating cannot: a concentrated antioxidant shield sitting directly on the surface, where UV and pollution actually make contact. That's genuine, and it's why vitamin C pairs so naturally with daily sun protection — the two work as a system.

What the study changes is the hierarchy. For fifteen years the serum was positioned as the whole strategy. It was always the finish. The foundation was on your plate, and almost nobody mentioned it, because you cannot put a 60% margin on a kiwifruit.

Use both. Just stop expecting the bottle to do work that only the bloodstream can reach.

How to Choose a Serum That Actually Works

If you're buying one — and you should — there are only a few things that genuinely matter, and none of them are the packaging.

01

L-ascorbic acid, not a vague "vitamin C complex"

L-ascorbic acid is the most researched and most bioavailable form for skin. Derivatives exist and some are gentler, but the evidence base sits overwhelmingly with the original.

02

Between 10% and 20%

Below roughly 10% tends to underdeliver. Above about 20% buys you irritation rather than results. The sweet spot is unfashionably moderate, which is why brands rarely shout about it.

03

Vitamin E and ferulic acid on the ingredients list

This pairing is the genuinely meaningful formulation detail. Both stabilise ascorbic acid and extend its antioxidant performance — which matters enormously, because pure vitamin C is famously unstable.

04

Opaque, airtight, and not already orange

Vitamin C oxidises on contact with light and air. If your serum has turned amber or brown, it has already degraded — you're applying the spent version. Dark glass and a pump beat a clear bottle with a dropper every time.

When to Apply It — Morning or Night?

Morning. This is the one timing question worth being firm about, and the logic is simple: vitamin C's antioxidant work is defensive, and the assault it defends against — UV, pollution, daylight — happens while you're awake. Applying it at bedtime is like putting on a raincoat to go to sleep.

The order is unfussy. Cleanse, then vitamin C onto clean skin while it's still slightly damp, then let it settle for a minute, then moisturiser, then SPF last. It goes early in the sequence because it's a thin, water-based layer and anything heavier applied first will block it. I've laid out the full layering order and when to let skin rest if you want the complete sequence.

On the pairing question everyone asks: vitamin C and niacinamide together is fine. The old warning that they cancel each other out traces back to decades-old research using unstable raw ingredients under heat — not modern formulations. Contemporary products are formulated to coexist, and plenty combine both deliberately.

If you have sensitive skin, or skin that reacts easily, start at a lower concentration, apply every other morning, and build from there. Some initial tingling is common; stinging, persistent redness or burning is not, and means the concentration is too high for you. That's information, not failure — and a gentler derivative may suit you better than L-ascorbic acid.

The Foundation: What to Actually Eat

Here's the part that costs almost nothing, which is precisely why no one has been marketing it to you.

Vitamin C is water-soluble and isn't stored long-term. Your blood levels rise and fall with what you ate that day — which means this is a consistency game, not a heroic-dose game. A single enormous serving on Sunday does very little for the skin you're wearing on Thursday.

The Highest-Vitamin-C Foods for Skin

Most people assume oranges lead this list. Most people are wrong.

  • Kiwifruit — the study's chosen fruit, and one of the most concentrated and reliable sources available year-round.
  • Red and yellow bell peppers — gram for gram, higher than citrus. Raw, in a salad, they outperform an orange comfortably.
  • Guava and blackcurrants — the quiet overachievers, if you can get them.
  • Strawberries — a generous handful delivers roughly what a medium orange does, with far less sugar per serving.
  • Broccoli and Brussels sprouts — genuinely high, but heat-sensitive. Steam briefly; boiling leaches the vitamin C straight into the water you pour away.
  • Leafy greens and citrus — reliable, everyday, and best eaten fresh rather than after a week in the fridge.

Two practical notes the study points toward. First, the useful amount is meaningfully higher than the bare minimum that prevents deficiency — the participants were eating two vitamin C–rich kiwifruit daily, not a token slice of lemon in hot water. Second, vitamin C degrades with heat, light and storage, so fresh and raw or lightly cooked will always beat something that's been sitting out for a fortnight.

If the idea of eating for your skin interests you beyond this one nutrient, the wider produce-aisle approach to glow covers the rest.

How Long Before Anything Changes

Eight weeks in the kiwifruit study. Twelve in most topical firmness research. That's your honest window either way — two to three months of unremarkable daily consistency.

I say this because the beauty industry has trained us to expect a visible result by Friday, and collagen simply does not work on that schedule. Anything promising visible firming in seven days is delivering hydration, which plumps temporarily and beautifully and disappears the moment you stop. Structure is slower. Structure is also the thing that lasts.
