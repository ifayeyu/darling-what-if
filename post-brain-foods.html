The Best and Worst Foods for Your Brain

November 24, 2026
By Faye Livia
9 min read

One study found that people eating a single serving of leafy greens a day had brains performing like those of people eleven years younger. The serving in question is about half a cup of cooked spinach. That is a genuinely absurd return on investment.

Your brain is the most expensive thing you own.

It accounts for roughly 2% of your body weight and burns through something like 20% of your daily energy — a demanding little organ that never clocks off, running continuously on whatever fuel you hand it. It's also largely built of fat, which is why the fat in your diet isn't a side issue for cognition. It's construction material.

Which leads to a slightly unnerving thought: the brain you're using to read this sentence was assembled, in part, from things you ate. The groceries became the machinery.

So the question of which foods actually support it isn't a wellness affectation. It's maintenance on the most important asset you have. Here's what the research genuinely shows — including the parts the internet tends to leave out.

The Finding That Should Be Better Known

In Chicago, researchers at Rush University ran something called the Memory and Aging Project, following around 960 older adults and tracking both what they ate and how their cognition changed over time.

When they analysed the results, one food group separated itself from everything else — and it wasn't an exotic berry or an expensive oil.

11 years
Participants eating roughly one serving of green leafy vegetables per day showed cognitive performance comparable to people about eleven years younger. Leafy greens remained the single most significant dietary factor even after adjusting for age, physical activity, alcohol and smoking (Morris et al., Rush Memory and Aging Project).

And the dose is the part that makes it almost silly. One serving means roughly one and a third cups of raw lettuce — a side salad — or a little over half a cup of cooked dark greens. A handful of spinach wilted into whatever was already in the pan.

Not a supplement. Not a protocol. Half a cup of spinach.

We will happily spend forty pounds on a serum for our face and won't spend ninety pence on a bag of spinach for the organ that holds every memory we have.

What the MIND Diet Actually Is

That research came out of work on the MIND diet — Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay — developed by the late nutritional epidemiologist Martha Clare Morris and colleagues.

It's a hybrid of two well-studied patterns, and crucially it is a pattern, not a superfood list. It emphasises ten brain-healthy groups: green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, berries, nuts, beans, whole grains, fish, poultry and olive oil. It limits red meat, butter, cheese, pastries, sweets, and fried or fast food.

Nothing there is surprising, which is rather the point. The interesting part isn't the novelty. It's that when researchers followed people actually eating this way, the cognitive differences were measurable.

The Five That Earn Their Place

If you strip the lists down to what has the most evidence behind it, you end up with a fairly short shopping list.

01

Green leafy vegetables

Spinach, kale, collards, chard, romaine. The most consistently protective food in the research, delivering folate, vitamin K, lutein and nitrates that support blood flow. If you change one thing, change this one.

02

Fatty fish, for the DHA

Salmon, sardines, mackerel, anchovies. DHA is a structural component of brain cell membranes — you are literally building with it. Smaller fish carry the added advantage of accumulating less mercury than the large predators.

03

Berries

Blueberries have the most research behind them, largely for their anthocyanins — the pigments that make them that colour. Frozen work perfectly well and cost a fraction of fresh, which matters if this is going to be a habit rather than a gesture.

04

Nuts, particularly walnuts

A source of plant omega-3 (ALA) plus vitamin E and polyphenols. And yes — walnuts do look uncannily like little brains. That's a coincidence, not a signature, however much the internet would like it to be otherwise.

05

Beans and lentils

Deeply unfashionable and quietly excellent: fibre, folate, plant protein, and slow-release carbohydrate that keeps blood sugar steady. Stable glucose matters more for everyday mental clarity than almost any single ingredient.

Extra credit for extra virgin olive oil, eggs (the yolk carries choline, used to build the neurotransmitter acetylcholine), green tea, dark chocolate at 70% or above, and fermented foods — which reach the brain by a different route entirely, through the gut-brain connection.

And Now the Other Side

The "worst foods" half of this conversation usually dissolves into vague finger-wagging about sugar. There is one finding that deserves better than that.

28%
In a 2022 study published in JAMA Neurology, following around 11,000 adults over roughly a decade, those taking more than 20% of daily calories from ultra-processed foods showed a 28% faster rate of overall cognitive decline, with executive function declining faster too.

Twenty percent of calories is a lower bar than it sounds. On a 2,000-calorie day that's 400 calories — a pastry and a packet of crisps, and you're there before dinner.

The usual suspects sit alongside it: sugar-sweetened drinks, very high sodium intake, and diets heavy in saturated fat, all of which are consistently associated with poorer cognitive outcomes. I've written separately about what actually changes when you cut ultra-processed food, because the effects reach well beyond the brain.

What I'd resist is the moralising version of this. Nobody's memory is destroyed by a biscuit. It's the proportion of the diet that the research measures — and proportion is a far more forgiving thing to work with than perfection.

The Part Most Articles Leave Out

I want to be straight with you about the strength of this evidence, because I think being trusted matters more than being exciting.

Nearly all of the striking findings above are observational. They track what people eat and what happens to them, which means they can show a strong association but can't fully prove causation. People who eat plenty of leafy greens tend to differ in other ways too — more exercise, more sleep, more money, better healthcare.

And when the MIND diet was put to the harder test — a randomised controlled trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2023 — the cognitive benefits over three years turned out to be modest, and not clearly better than the control diet.

So the honest summary is this. Diet is one real factor among several, and probably not the largest. Sleep, exercise, blood pressure, hearing loss and social connection all have serious evidence behind them too. No plate of salmon is a shield against dementia.

But here's what makes the decision easy anyway: every food on that list is independently excellent for your heart, your blood sugar and your gut. The downside risk of eating more spinach is zero. That's a rare position to be in.

What About Supplements?

The obvious question, and the honest answer is less satisfying than the marketing.

Omega-3 supplements are the most studied, and the results are genuinely mixed — trials in people who already have adequate intake have often failed to show meaningful cognitive benefit. That doesn't mean DHA is unimportant; it means a capsule doesn't reliably replicate what a dietary pattern does. Food arrives with a supporting cast that pills don't have.

Vitamin D is the clearest exception, because deficiency is common, it's genuinely associated with low mood, and it's difficult to get enough from food or winter daylight alone. That one is worth a blood test rather than a guess.

Beyond that, you'll find claims for curcumin, citicoline, acetyl-L-carnitine and various nootropic blends. Some have interesting early research. None has the weight of evidence behind it that half a cup of spinach does, and all of them cost considerably more.

The order of operations matters here: sort the food first, then supplement what you've actually tested for.

The Things That Matter More Than Food

I think any honest article about brain health has to say this part, even though it's slightly against interest for a food piece.

In 2024, the Lancet Commission on dementia prevention published an updated analysis identifying 14 modifiable risk factors which, if all were eliminated, could potentially prevent around 45% of dementia cases worldwide.

The list is instructive, and diet is not the headline act. It includes hearing loss, high blood pressure, smoking, physical inactivity, diabetes, depression, social isolation, excessive alcohol, traumatic brain injury, air pollution, low education, obesity, and — newly added — high LDL cholesterol in midlife and untreated vision loss.

Untreated hearing loss is one of the largest single contributors. Which means that, on the current evidence, getting a hearing test in your fifties may do more for your long-term cognition than any amount of blueberries.

That isn't a reason to ignore your plate. Diet feeds into several of those factors directly — blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, weight. But it's a reason to be suspicious of any article, including this one, that implies your fork is the whole story.

What I'd Actually Do

The Unglamorous Version

Consistency beats intensity. Every time.

  • Get greens in daily, by stealth. A handful of spinach disappears into eggs, soup, pasta sauce, a smoothie. You don't need to enjoy salad to clear the bar.
  • Fish twice a week, and let the tinned stuff count. Sardines on toast is a legitimate brain-health intervention and costs almost nothing.
  • Keep frozen berries in the freezer. They don't rot while you feel guilty about them. That alone makes them more effective than the fresh ones.
  • Deal with the drinks first. Sugar-sweetened drinks are usually the single largest, easiest-to-remove source of ultra-processed calories in a diet.
  • Drink water before you diagnose yourself with brain fog. Mild dehydration measurably affects concentration, and it's the cheapest thing on this list.
  • Aim for proportion, not purity. The research measures the shape of an overall diet, not individual moral failures on a Friday night.

One last thing worth saying: persistent brain fog isn't always a nutrition question. It's a common feature of anaemia, thyroid problems, poor sleep and a number of other entirely treatable conditions. If the fog is constant rather than occasional, that's a conversation for your GP and a blood test — not a bag of blueberries.
