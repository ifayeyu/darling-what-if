What "Husband Material" Actually Means

September 15, 2026
By Faye Livia
9 min read

"Husband material" gets thrown around constantly, and most of the time it quietly means one thing: a good paycheck. That's not what the research says actually predicts a good marriage — and it's worth naming the difference before you're years into finding out the hard way.

Call someone husband material, and most people hear one thing: money. A number in an account, a certain kind of stability, the version of wealth that shows up in a listing price or a watch. It's not an unreasonable thing to want. Financial stability is real, and pretending it doesn't matter does no one any favors.

But there's another kind of rich, and it's the one that actually determines whether a marriage survives its harder years — not the first ten thrilling ones, but the ones with a sick parent, a lost job, a move nobody wanted to make, a year that just goes sideways. Money can help you weather those years. It has never, on its own, been the thing that gets two people through them.

That's the wealth worth actually screening for — not instead of financial stability, but alongside it, and arguably ahead of it, because it's the kind that doesn't disappear the moment the money gets tight.

Seven Kinds of Wealth That Actually Hold Up

None of these are about how impressive someone looks on paper. They're about what's actually available to you on an ordinary Tuesday, and on the much harder days that eventually show up in every long relationship.

01

Rich in health

The daily, unglamorous discipline of taking care of a body and mind. Someone who neglects his own health isn't just making a private choice — he's quietly signing you up to become his caretaker sooner than either of you planned.

02

Rich in humor

Not just funny on a good night. The kind of humor that survives an argument — someone who can find something to laugh about with you after a hard day, not only during an easy one.

03

Rich in kindness

How he treats a waiter, a stranger, an ex he's describing to you. Kindness that only appears when it's being observed isn't character — it's performance, and it tends to run out exactly when you need it most.

04

Rich in romance

Not grand gestures on a schedule, but ongoing, unglamorous effort — choosing you again in the small moments, not just the anniversaries where everyone's watching.

05

Rich in adventure

A willingness to stay curious with you — try the new restaurant, take the weird road, keep some appetite for the unfamiliar instead of settling into routine before the relationship has even had a chance to.

06

Rich in responsibility

Does what he says he'll do, without needing to be managed into it. It sounds unromantic. It is, in practice, one of the most underrated forms of love there is.

07

Rich in emotional presence

Shows up. Listens. Stays in the room during a hard conversation instead of shutting down or checking out. Support, patience, and genuinely sharing a life all live inside this one.

Notice what's missing from that list: net worth, job title, the size of a ring. None of that is irrelevant to a life together — but none of it has ever been the thing that predicts whether two people are still glad they chose each other twenty years in.

The Research Behind This

This isn't just a nice sentiment. Psychologist John Gottman has spent decades studying married couples, tracking what actually happens between two people, minute to minute, and following up years later to see who stayed together and who didn't. One of his most striking findings involves what he calls "bids for connection" — the small, constant moments where one partner reaches for the other's attention, and the other either turns toward it or away.

86% vs 33%
In one of Gottman's newlywed studies, couples still happily married six years later had turned toward each other's everyday bids for connection about 86% of the time. Couples who later divorced had done so only about 33% of the time (The Gottman Institute).

That gap — 86% versus 33% — isn't about income, education, or how a couple looked on their wedding day. It's about whether one partner's small reach for attention, humor, comfort, or help gets met or ignored. Every item on the list above is, in one way or another, a version of that: a partner who is rich in emotional presence turns toward those bids. A partner who is rich in kindness and responsibility makes it safe to keep reaching in the first place.

The richest partner isn't the one with the most to give you. It's the one who keeps giving, in the ordinary moments, long after anyone's watching.

Why This Doesn't Mean Money Doesn't Matter

It would be a little dishonest to pretend financial stability is beside the point — money stress is one of the most common, most researched sources of conflict in marriages, and pretending otherwise sets couples up for a fight they didn't need to have. The point isn't that money is irrelevant. It's that money is the wrong primary filter, because it doesn't predict what you actually need it to predict.

A relationship can survive being broke. The research, and most people's lived experience, suggests it has a much harder time surviving contempt, dishonesty, or a partner who simply isn't there when it counts. Screen for the seven kinds of wealth first. Let financial compatibility be a real, reasonable, second conversation — not the whole conversation.

How to Tell If You're Actually Building This

These aren't just traits to look for in someone else. Every item on this list is something you can practice too, which is good news — this isn't a fixed test someone passes or fails on the first date. It's a pattern that either gets built, together, or doesn't.

A Few Honest Questions to Ask

About a specific relationship, or about yourself.

  • When one of you reaches for the other — for attention, comfort, or help — does it usually get met? That's the "bids" question, and it's a better predictor than almost anything else on this list.
  • How does he treat people who can't do anything for him? That's the real kindness test, not how he treats you on a good day.
  • Does he follow through on the small things, or only the ones you'll notice? Responsibility shows up in the unglamorous, unwatched moments first.
  • Can you still laugh together after a hard conversation, not just before one? That's the difference between humor as decoration and humor as a real resource.

Real husband material was never measured in dollars.

A life built on character, care, and emotional abundance will outlast almost anything a bank account can promise you. Not because money doesn't help — it does — but because it was never the thing keeping two people in the room with each other on the hardest days. That was always going to come from somewhere else.
