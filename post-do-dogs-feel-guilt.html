Do Dogs Feel Guilt? The Study That Changes How You'll See That Face

August 2, 2026
By Faye Livia
11 min read

You know the face. Everyone knows the face. It turns out we have all been reading it backwards — and the mistake is costing our dogs something.

You open the door. Before you've seen the shredded post, the tipped bin, the shoe, you already know — because your dog is doing the thing.

Ears flat. Head low. Eyes sliding away from yours. A tail moving in slow apologetic arcs near the floor. She's already halfway to the other room, walking as though the carpet might give way.

He knows what he did. Everyone says it. It's the most confidently repeated claim in dog ownership.

In 2009 a cognitive scientist at Barnard College decided to actually test it. The result is one of the most quietly upsetting findings in canine research — not because it makes dogs seem less than we thought, but because of what it says about what we've been doing to them.

The short answer

No — not guilt as humans experience it. The "guilty look" is a reaction to you, not to the crime. Dogs produce it whether or not they've actually done anything wrong.

The Experiment That Settled It

Alexandra Horowitz built her study around a simple, slightly devious idea: separate what the dog did from what the owner believed.

Each owner told their dog to leave a treat alone, then left the room. While they were gone, the experimenter either let the dog eat the treat or quietly removed it. Then the owner came back — and here's the clever part. Sometimes they were told the truth about what had happened. Sometimes they were told the opposite.

So you end up with four situations.

Dog obeyed · owner told the truth

Owner is pleased

Greeting is warm. The dog is relaxed, loose, unbothered. No guilty look.

Dog disobeyed · owner told the truth

Owner scolds

Ears back, head down, gaze away. Guilty look present — exactly as everyone expects.

Dog disobeyed · owner told they obeyed

Owner is pleased anyway

The dog genuinely ate the treat — and behaves normally. No guilty look, despite the "crime."

Dog obeyed · owner told they disobeyed

Owner scolds an innocent dog

Ears flat, body low, slinking. The guiltiest look of the entire study — from a dog who did nothing.

Read that last box again.

The dogs who looked guiltiest were the ones who had been perfectly obedient and got told off anyway.

The expression didn't track the behaviour. It tracked the owner's face. A dog who'd eaten the treat but was greeted warmly acted innocent; a dog who'd resisted temptation but was greeted with a scolding acted like she'd committed a felony.

The guilty look isn't a confession. It's a prediction — of what your face is about to do next.

So What Is That Face?

It has a name in animal behaviour, and once you know it you'll never unsee it: appeasement.

These are ancient signals in social canids, and they all say roughly the same thing: you are upset, I am not a threat, please let this stop. They exist to prevent conflict inside a group — and they work, because they're extremely hard to be angry at.

What makes dogs unusual is how precisely these signals are aimed at us. Decades of research from Ádám Miklósi's Family Dog Project in Budapest — one of the largest bodies of work on canine social cognition anywhere — has shown that dogs are exceptionally attuned to human communicative cues, following our pointing and gaze in ways even hand-raised wolves largely don't. Your dog isn't guessing at your mood. She has an evolved specialism in reading it.

01

Ears flattened, head lowered

Making the body smaller and less confrontational. The opposite of a challenge.

02

Gaze averted — or whale eye

Direct eye contact is pressure in dog language. She looks away, or keeps you in sight sideways with the whites showing. That's discomfort, not shiftiness.

03

Low, slow tail sweep

Not the loose happy wag. A tail held low, moving in tight anxious arcs — often misread as a guilty wag.

04

Lip licking and yawning

With no food and no tiredness in sight. These are calming signals — small self-soothing gestures that also broadcast I'm no trouble.

05

Slinking, or rolling over

Moving low and slow, or exposing the belly. Not an apology — a request for the tension to end.

Why Dogs Can't Feel Guilt (Probably)

This isn't a claim that dogs are emotionally shallow. They're not.

Researchers broadly accept that dogs experience primary emotions — joy, fear, anger, disgust, excitement, contentment, distress — and there's strong evidence for affection and attachment. Anyone who's lived with a dog knows this without needing a citation.

What's disputed are the self-conscious emotions: guilt, shame, pride, embarrassment. These require something extra — a concept of yourself, measured against a standard you've internalised. Guilt isn't just "something bad happened." It's I did a thing I understood to be wrong, and I am the kind of creature who shouldn't have.

That's a lot of scaffolding. You'll often see a dog's emotional range compared to that of a human toddler of around two to two and a half — a comparison popularised by the psychologist Stanley Coren. It's a useful picture rather than a precise measurement, but it captures the shape of the thing: a rich, real, fully-felt inner life, arriving just before the machinery for guilt comes online.

The honest counterargument

I should say that not everyone accepts this, and the dissent is worth taking seriously.

The biologist Marc Bekoff has argued for years that researchers are too quick to rule out complex emotions in animals — that we demand a standard of proof for animal feelings we'd never demand for human ones, and that absence of evidence keeps getting treated as evidence of absence. On his view, something guilt-adjacent may well exist in dogs; we simply haven't designed an experiment capable of finding it.

It's a fair point, and it doesn't undo Horowitz's finding. Her study didn't prove dogs can't feel guilt. It proved that the specific face we've been reading as guilt isn't reliable evidence of it — because it appears just as readily in dogs who did nothing wrong.

Which is enough for the practical question this article is actually about. Whatever is happening in your dog's inner life, that expression is not a confession, and treating it as one changes how you behave in ways that don't help either of you.

Seconds
Not minutes, and certainly not "when I got home." Associative learning depends on close timing between behaviour and consequence — a principle established across decades of learning research, not specific to dogs. Trainers generally work to a rule of thumb of a second or two. The exact number is a practical guideline rather than a precise finding, but the direction is not in dispute, and it's the reason after-the-fact telling off doesn't teach what people think it teaches.

What Your Dog Actually Learned From the Shoe Incident

Here's where this stops being trivia and starts mattering.

You come home three hours after the shoe was destroyed. You find it, you're annoyed, and your dog does the face. You tell her off. She looks even guiltier. You conclude the message landed.

It didn't. Because she cannot connect your anger to something she did three hours ago, the association she forms is with the thing that's happening right now — which is you, in the doorway, upset.

Do this often enough and what you build is a dog who is anxious about your homecoming. Not a dog who's stopped chewing shoes.

And here's the cruel loop: anxiety is one of the most common causes of destructive chewing in the first place. So the punishment can quietly feed the behaviour it was meant to end. The dog gets more anxious, chews more, is punished more, looks guiltier — and the owner reads that growing guilt as proof the dog knows better and simply won't stop.

She isn't sorry about the shoe. She's frightened of your face. And every time you read that fear as an apology, you accept an apology she never made and teach her something you never meant to.

What to Do Instead

The good news: the fix is easier than the punishment, and it works.

The Trainer's Version

Managing the environment beats correcting the dog, every single time.

  • If you didn't catch it, say nothing. Clean it up calmly. Any reaction now is landing on the wrong moment. This will feel unsatisfying and it is the correct thing to do.
  • Change the room, not the dog. Bin behind a door. Shoes off the floor. Baby gate on the hallway. You will win this contest far more reliably through logistics than through discipline.
  • If you do catch it — interrupt, don't punish. A neutral "ah-ah," then hand her something she is allowed to chew. Then praise her for taking it. That's the actual lesson.
  • Reward the boring behaviour. Almost nobody does this. When she's lying quietly next to the shoes doing nothing, that's the moment worth marking. Undramatic behaviour needs paying for too.
  • Add exercise and enrichment before adding rules. A great deal of household destruction is an under-stimulated dog solving her own boredom. A sniffing walk and a puzzle feeder fix more chewing than any correction.
  • Watch your own arrival. If she's already appeasing before you've seen anything, your face is doing more talking than you realise. Come in flat and calm. She's reading you from the sound of the key.
What You Think You're Teaching
  • "Don't chew shoes" — A rule about objects, understood and remembered.
  • "You know that was wrong" — A moral point, landing.
  • "That look means she's sorry" — An apology, accepted.
  • "She'll think twice next time" — Deterrence, working.
What She's Actually Learning
  • "Sometimes she comes home angry" — About your return, not the shoe.
  • "I can't tell what causes it" — Because from her side, it's unpredictable.
  • "Getting small makes it stop" — Appeasement works, so it strengthens.
  • "Being alone feels bad" — Which is what causes the chewing to begin with.

When destruction isn't mischief

Some chewing is boredom. Some is something else, and it's worth knowing the difference — because no amount of training fixes a panic attack.

Signs that point to separation anxiety rather than naughtiness:

  • Destruction concentrated at exit points — doors, window frames, the hallway — rather than random objects
  • Damage that starts within the first fifteen to thirty minutes of you leaving
  • Drooling, panting, pacing, or house-soiling in an otherwise reliably clean dog
  • Howling or barking that continues rather than settling
  • Distress that begins before you go, at the sight of keys or shoes

If that sounds familiar, this is a welfare issue rather than a discipline one, and it responds well to proper help. Speak to your vet and ask for a referral to a qualified, force-free behaviourist. Punishment makes separation anxiety measurably worse.

The Part That Stayed With Me

What I keep coming back to is the fourth box in that experiment. The dog who did everything right. Who was told to leave the treat and left it — and then got scolded anyway, because her owner had been given the wrong information.

She had no idea why. She had no way to ask. So she did the only thing available: made herself small, looked away, and waited for it to be over.

And her owner, watching, thought: look how guilty she is.

That's the whole thing, really. For as long as we've lived with dogs, we've been reading fear as remorse — and feeling reassured by it, because remorse implies understanding, and understanding feels like closeness.

The truth is closer than that, and sadder, and better. She isn't tracking a moral code. She's tracking you. Constantly, carefully, with an attention no human in your life gives you. Your face is the most important thing in her world, and when it changes, she changes.

Which means the next time you walk in and find something ruined, you get to decide what she learns. Not about the shoe — that lesson was never going to land. About you, and whether the door opening is a good thing or a frightening one.

She's not sorry. She's worried about you.

The face you've been reading as guilt is your dog saying please don't be angry, I don't know what's happening, I love you. Not a confession — a small anxious animal trying to keep the peace with the person she loves most. So put the shoes away, let the bin have a lid, and let her off. She never knew she was on trial in the first place.

The central study is Alexandra Horowitz, "Disambiguating the 'guilty look': Salient prompts to a familiar dog behaviour" (Behavioural Processes, 2009). Her book Inside of a Dog is the best general introduction to how dogs perceive the world. A follow-up by Hecht, Miklósi & Gácsi (Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2012) reached broadly consistent conclusions using a different design.

On canine social cognition — the Family Dog Project at Eötvös Loránd University, led by Ádám Miklósi, has produced much of the modern research on how dogs read human cues. On the other side of the argument, biologist Marc Bekoff (The Emotional Lives of Animals) argues that science sets an unreasonably high bar for animal emotion and that complex feelings in dogs shouldn't be ruled out — a position I've tried to represent fairly rather than dismiss.

Two claims worth flagging as rules of thumb rather than findings: the "seconds, not minutes" learning window is a training convention grounded in general associative-learning research rather than a specific canine study, and the toddler comparison is a popularisation associated with Stanley Coren, not a measured equivalence.

Appeasement and calming signals were first catalogued by Norwegian trainer Turid Rugaas in On Talking Terms With Dogs. Guidance on separation anxiety and force-free handling aligns with the position statements of the American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior. If your dog is destroying things when left alone, or showing distress before you leave, please speak to your vet rather than working from an article — including this one.

Questions About Dogs and Guilt

Almost certainly not in the way humans mean it. Guilt requires understanding that you broke a rule you'd accepted, and reflecting on yourself as the one who broke it — a level of moral self-awareness dogs aren't generally thought to possess. What we call the guilty look is better understood as appeasement behaviour. In a well-known 2009 study, Alexandra Horowitz at Barnard College found the guilty look appeared based on whether the owner scolded the dog rather than whether the dog had actually disobeyed. Dogs who'd been perfectly obedient but were told off anyway showed the most guilty behaviour of all.

It's a set of appeasement signals: ears flattened, head and body lowered, gaze averted, tail tucked or sweeping low, sometimes lip licking, yawning or rolling over. In social canids these signals communicate something close to you're upset, I'm not a threat, please stop. Your dog isn't confessing to the chewed shoe — she's reading your face, your posture and your tone, concluding you're angry, and attempting to defuse the situation as quickly as possible.

Your dog may well know that a particular behaviour has previously led to trouble — that's associative learning rather than moral understanding. What dogs struggle with is connecting a consequence to an action that happened much earlier. If you come home hours after the bin was raided and scold your dog, the association they form isn't about the bin. It's about your arrival. Dogs learn best when feedback arrives within a second or two of the behaviour, which is why after-the-fact telling off teaches almost nothing useful.

It's ineffective and can be counterproductive. Because the dog can't link your anger to something they did hours earlier, what they learn instead is that your homecoming is unpredictable and sometimes frightening. Over time this can produce anxiety around your return, more appeasement behaviour that owners then misread as increasing guilt, and in some cases an increase in the destructive behaviour itself — since anxiety is a common driver of chewing and toileting indoors. Managing the environment works far better than punishing after the event.

Researchers generally accept that dogs experience primary emotions including joy, fear, anger, disgust, excitement, contentment and distress, and there's good evidence for affection and attachment. What remains debated are the self-conscious or secondary emotions — guilt, shame, pride, embarrassment — because these appear to require a concept of self being evaluated against a standard. A common comparison is that a dog's emotional range resembles that of a human toddler of around two to two and a half: rich and real, but without the layer of self-reflection guilt depends on.

If you didn't catch them in the act, the most useful response is to say nothing, clean it up calmly, and change the environment so it can't happen again — bin behind a door, shoes away, a crate or safe room, and more physical and mental exercise. If you do catch them in the act, a neutral interruption followed by redirection to something they're allowed to have works better than anger. Repeated destruction when left alone, particularly alongside pacing, drooling, howling or toileting, may indicate separation anxiety and is worth discussing with a vet or a qualified behaviourist.

There's no good evidence that they do, and the same reasoning applies as with guilt. Shame and embarrassment are self-conscious emotions requiring awareness of how one appears to others against a social standard. What people usually interpret as an embarrassed dog is a dog responding to laughter, sudden noise, unfamiliar clothing, restricted movement, or the general strangeness of a situation. The behaviour is real and worth respecting — but the emotion behind it is more likely discomfort or mild stress than self-conscious shame.
