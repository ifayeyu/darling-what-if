What If Nothing Bad Happened — and It Still Shaped You?

August 2, 2026
By Faye Livia
12 min read

You have spent years looking for the thing that explains it, and finding nothing. That is not because there's nothing there. It's because you've been looking for an event.

Someone asks about your childhood and you say it was fine.

You mean it. There was no violence. Nobody drank. Your parents stayed together, or they didn't and it was handled reasonably. You were fed, clothed, driven to things. Photographs exist of birthdays. By any measure you can name, you were fortunate — and you know people who weren't, and you feel faintly ridiculous even circling this question.

And yet.

There is a persistent difficulty you cannot account for. You struggle to say what you feel, or you say "fine" and realise you have no idea whether it's true. You find it almost unbearable to need something from someone. You are decent and patient with everyone you know and startlingly cruel to yourself. There is, sometimes, a flat quality to things — not sadness exactly, more an absence where you assume other people have something.

You have gone looking for the cause. You have combed through your childhood for the incident. And you've come back with nothing, which you've quietly taken as proof that the problem must simply be you.

It isn't. You've been searching for something that happened. The thing you're looking for is something that didn't.

The Absence Has a Name

Psychologist Dr Jonice Webb gave it one: childhood emotional neglect — a parent's consistent failure to respond adequately to a child's emotional needs. Her book Running on Empty brought it into wide circulation, and its central insight is deceptively simple. It has a mirror image worth knowing about — enmeshment, where the problem isn't a parent's absence but a closeness that never left room for you to be separate.

Almost everything we recognise as childhood harm is an act of commission. Something was done. Somebody shouted, hit, humiliated, disappeared, drank. Those things leave memories, and memories can be examined, discussed, worked through.

Emotional neglect is an act of omission. Nothing was done. Nobody asked how you felt. Nobody noticed you'd gone quiet. Nobody sat on the edge of the bed and waited while you found the words. The response you needed simply never arrived — reliably, over years, in a house where everyone was perfectly pleasant.

Which produces the defining problem of the whole thing:

You cannot remember an absence. There is no scene to return to, no moment to point at. Just a shape in your adult life where something should have been, and no memory of the thing that made it.

This is why people miss it for thirty years. It's also why the recognition, when it comes, is often physical — people describe reading a list of signs and starting to cry about a childhood in which, by their own account, nothing bad ever happened.

Why the Usual Measures Don't Catch It

If you've read anything about childhood adversity, you've probably encountered the ACE questionnaire — the Adverse Childhood Experiences study, which links early hardship to health outcomes across a lifetime. It's genuinely important research.

It also cannot see this.

The ACE questionnaire counts events: abuse, neglect of physical needs, household dysfunction, separation, substance use, incarceration. It's designed to record things that occurred. An absence isn't a thing that occurred, so it doesn't register — which means a person can carry a substantial history of emotional neglect and score zero.

Many people do exactly that. They take the test, score zero, and file the conclusion away: nothing happened to me, so this is just how I am. The instrument is fine. It simply wasn't built to detect a non-event.

0
The score many emotionally neglected adults receive on standard childhood adversity questionnaires. Not because nothing shaped them, but because the shaping took the form of an absence — and absences leave no entry on a checklist. This is, for a great many people, the exact moment the search for an explanation stops.

Eight Signs in Adults

These are the patterns most consistently described. A note before you read them: recognising several does not constitute a diagnosis, and the goal here isn't to hand you a new label. It's to give you language for something you may have been carrying without any.

01

You genuinely don't know what you feel

Not evasion — an actual blank. Asked how you feel, you produce an assessment of the situation instead, because that's the question you know how to answer. Children learn to identify emotions by having them named and reflected back. Where nobody did that, the vocabulary was never built. You're not withholding. You were never taught to read the instrument.

02

There's an emptiness you can't explain

Not depression exactly, though it can shade into it. More a flatness — a hollow place that shows up in quiet moments and has no obvious cause. People describe it as watching their life through glass, or as a hum of nothing underneath an otherwise functional day. It is the most commonly reported experience of all, and the hardest to describe to anyone.

03

Needing something feels shameful

You would rather struggle alone than ask, even when asking is easy and the person would gladly help. Somewhere early on you learned that needs were an imposition — not because anyone said so, but because when you had one, nothing came. A child concludes, reasonably, that the need itself was the mistake. Thirty years later you're still declining help you're entitled to.

04

You're kind to everyone but yourself

A friend describes exactly your situation and you're gentle, generous, unhesitatingly fair. You do the same thing and the voice that arrives is contemptuous. This gap is one of the clearest markers there is: compassion was learned outward and never turned inward, because nobody modelled what it sounds like to be gentle with yourself.

05

You feel fundamentally different from other people

A sense of standing slightly outside — as though everyone else received a briefing you missed, and you've been improvising the whole time. It's not shyness or dislike of people. It's the specific loneliness of never having been fully known as a child, which becomes an assumption that being fully known isn't really on offer.

06

You can describe your childhood but not feel it

You can list what happened — schools, houses, holidays, the year your father changed jobs. Ask how any of it felt and there's very little. Many people notice they narrate their own early life in something close to the third person, like a report on someone else's childhood. The events were recorded. The emotional track wasn't.

07

You're privately furious with yourself for struggling

This one keeps people stuck longer than any other. Other people had real problems. I had a fine childhood. What is wrong with me. The absence of a story to justify the difficulty becomes further evidence of personal failure — which means the very thing that caused the wound also prevents you from taking it seriously.

08

You are extremely competent and quietly exhausted

Self-sufficiency arrived early, because it had to. You are the reliable one, the capable one, the person others lean on. It looks like strength and often is. But underneath there's a fatigue that rest doesn't touch — the tiredness of having been your own parent since you were nine.

Why It Happened — and Why It Wasn't About You

Here is the part that tends to arrive with the most relief, and it requires holding two things at once.

Emotional neglect is rarely a failure of love. It is almost always a failure of capacity.

A parent cannot give a child attunement they never received themselves. If your mother grew up in a house where feelings were an inconvenience — where you got on with things, where crying was met with impatience or simply with silence — then she reached adulthood without the equipment. Not the words, not the tolerance for another person's distress, not the instinct to ask a second question after "fine."

She may have loved you enormously. She may have worked herself to exhaustion for you, shown up to everything, sacrificed things you'll never know about. And she may still have been entirely unable to sit with you while you were sad, because nobody had ever done that with her, and you cannot hand over what you were never given.

This is why emotional neglect runs down family lines so quietly. Nobody intends it. It's simply the shape of what wasn't passed on, repeating.

Which means both of these are true, and you're allowed to hold them together: they did their best, and something you genuinely needed did not arrive. The second doesn't erase the first. Explaining is not excusing, and understanding why it happened does not oblige you to pretend it didn't cost you anything.

The strangest grief is grief for something that never happened. There's no funeral for the childhood you didn't have — only a slow, private recognition, usually somewhere in your thirties, of what you were owed and quietly went without.

The Hardest Part Nobody Warns You About

Once you see it, there's a stage that catches people off guard, and it's worth naming so it doesn't frighten you.

You have to grieve an absence. And absence is an almost impossible thing to mourn, because there's no object — no person who died, no relationship that ended, no event to mark. Just the growing awareness of a thing you needed, that was never there, that you can never now be given, because the window in which a child receives it has closed.

People often report feeling worse for a period after they first understand this. That's not the recognition going wrong. It's what happens when a feeling that was suppressed at five finally gets acknowledged at thirty-five — it arrives at its original volume, and it's disorienting.

It also passes. And on the other side is something most people don't expect: an enormous drop in self-blame. Because the moment this has a cause outside your character, the decades of quiet self-contempt lose their footing. You weren't broken. You were under-equipped, in a specific and identifiable way, for reasons that had nothing to do with your worth.

What You Concluded
  • "I'm too much." — Because expressing a feeling produced nothing, you decided the feeling was the problem.
  • "I shouldn't need help." — Because no help came, you learned to require none. It looked like maturity. It was adaptation.
  • "Something's wrong with me." — Because you couldn't locate a cause, you assumed you were the cause.
  • "Other people had it worse." — True, and irrelevant. Suffering isn't a competition with an entry requirement.
What Was Actually True
  • You were a child with normal needs. Every single one of them was developmentally appropriate.
  • The response was missing, not the need. Nothing about your wanting to be comforted was excessive.
  • The cause was outside you. A gap in what your parents could give — not a defect in what you are.
  • It's still learnable. This is the genuinely good news. Emotional skills are skills. They can be acquired late.

Where It Actually Starts to Change

The work here is unusually specific, and unusually doable, because it's a developmental task rather than a wound. You're not undoing damage. You're learning something on a delay.

Six Places to Begin

Small and repetitive, in that order. None of this is dramatic, which is exactly why it works.

  • Ask yourself what you're feeling — twice a day, badly. Set an alarm if you need to. "Something tight" or "a bit flat" is a perfectly good answer. You're not searching for accuracy; you're building a habit of turning inward at all. Precision comes later, on its own.
  • Practise asking for very small things. Not the big ask. Ask someone to pass you something. Ask a friend to choose the restaurant. Each unremarkable request that goes fine is evidence against a rule you formed at six.
  • Use the friend test on yourself. When the harsh voice starts, ask what you'd say to someone you love in exactly this position. Then say that, even if it feels false. Self-compassion is a script before it's a feeling.
  • Stop ranking your suffering. "Other people had it worse" is the sentence that keeps this in place. Someone always has. It has never once made anyone's difficulty less real.
  • Notice the moment you shut something down. There's usually a tiny internal click — a feeling starts and you close it. Just catch it. You don't have to open it yet. Seeing the mechanism is most of the work.
  • Consider working with someone. Emotion-focused therapy, schema therapy, and internal family systems all address this directly. But the mechanism is simpler than the modality: sitting weekly with a person who is consistently curious about your inner life is the attunement you didn't get. That's not a metaphor. It's the actual repair.

One Thing Worth Saying Plainly

If you've read this far and something has been quietly building in your chest, I want to say the thing directly, because nobody said it to you at the time.

You were not too much. You were not difficult. You were not dramatic or needy or a lot to handle. You were a child who felt things — as children do, as they are supposed to — and there was nobody available to help you make sense of them. So you did the only sensible thing a child can do in that situation. You stopped bringing them to anyone. Eventually, you stopped bringing them to yourself.

That was an intelligent adaptation to the room you were standing in. It kept things manageable. It probably made you the capable, self-contained, low-maintenance person people have relied on ever since.

But you're not in that room anymore. And the thing you turned off at six is still there, waiting to be turned back on — not because you're broken and require fixing, but because it was always yours, and no one ever told you that you were allowed to keep it.

You have not been failing at something everyone else finds easy.

You have been doing it without instructions. There's a real difference, and it changes everything about how you might treat yourself from here. The years spent searching your childhood for the incident that explains you weren't wasted — you were simply looking for the wrong kind of thing. Nothing bad happened. Something good didn't. And now that it has a name, it stops being your character and starts being something you can actually work with.

Questions About Childhood Emotional Neglect

Childhood emotional neglect describes a parent's consistent failure to respond adequately to a child's emotional needs. The term was popularised by psychologist Dr Jonice Webb in her book Running on Empty. What distinguishes it from other adverse childhood experiences is that it's an act of omission rather than commission — it's defined by what did not happen. Nobody shouted, nobody left, nobody hit anyone. The child's feelings simply weren't noticed, asked about, or responded to. Because there's no event to remember, it's one of the most difficult childhood experiences to recognise, and most people who experienced it describe their childhood as ordinary or even happy.

The most commonly described signs include difficulty identifying and naming your own feelings; a persistent sense of emptiness or numbness; discomfort with needing anything from anyone; guilt or shame about having emotions at all; unusually harsh self-criticism paired with real compassion for others; a sense of being fundamentally different or on the outside of things; and difficulty asking for help even when help is freely available. Many adults also report being unable to describe how their childhood felt, only what happened in it — a kind of third-person account of their own early life.

Emotional abuse is something done to a child: criticism, humiliation, manipulation, threats, or rage. It's an active harm, and it leaves memories. Emotional neglect is the absence of something that should have been present — attunement, curiosity, comfort, the sense of being known. It leaves no memories, because you can't remember something that didn't occur. This is why abuse is generally easier to identify and neglect is so often missed entirely. The two frequently occur together, but neglect can and often does exist in homes where nothing anyone would call abusive ever took place.

Yes — and this is the most common and most disorienting version of it. Emotional neglect isn't incompatible with love, provision, or presence. A parent can adore their child, attend every school event, keep them fed, safe and clothed, and still be unable to ask how they felt or sit with them while they were sad. Very often these parents were emotionally neglected themselves and were never given the vocabulary or capacity to pass on. The absence of attunement isn't the same as the absence of love — which is precisely why it takes so many people decades to name it.

Chronic emptiness or numbness with no identifiable cause is one of the most frequently reported experiences among adults who were emotionally neglected. The likely explanation is developmental: children learn to identify, name and regulate emotions through a caregiver reflecting those emotions back to them. Where that mirroring didn't happen, feelings were often suppressed early as a practical adaptation — they weren't useful, and expressing them changed nothing. The feelings don't disappear, but access to them becomes limited, and the result can be a flat or hollow quality to inner life. Worth noting: persistent emptiness can also indicate depression or other conditions, so it's genuinely worth discussing with a professional rather than self-diagnosing.

The Adverse Childhood Experiences questionnaire measures discrete events — abuse, household dysfunction, parental separation, substance use, incarceration. It's built to count things that happened. Emotional neglect, by definition, is the absence of something, and an absence can't be recorded as an event. As a result many people with a significant history of emotional neglect score zero or near-zero and conclude their childhood was fine. The instrument is genuinely valuable for what it measures; it simply wasn't designed to detect a non-event.

Healing tends to begin with naming, because most people spend years assuming the problem is a flaw in their character rather than a gap in what they were taught. From there, the work is largely the developmental task that was missed: learning to notice, name, and tolerate your own feelings. Practically, this often means pausing several times a day to ask what you're feeling and accepting a vague answer, treating your own needs as legitimate rather than excessive, and practising asking for small things before large ones. Therapy is genuinely useful here — emotion-focused, schema, and internal family systems approaches are all commonly recommended — and simply having another person consistently curious about your inner life provides the attunement that was missing.
